## 📑 Key Methods
*   **Eliminating *t*:** Solve for *t* in the `x` equation, then plug it into the `y` equation.
*   **Trig Identity Method:** If you see `sin(t)` and `cos(t)`, use `sin²t + cos²t = 1` to find a circle or ellipse.

## 🛠️ Practice These Problems
1.  Convert `x = 2t + 1, y = t²` into a Cartesian equation.
2.  Convert `x = 3cos(t), y = 3sin(t)` into a Cartesian equation (Hint: It's a circle).
3.  Sketch the graph of `x = t + 1, y = 2t - 1` for `0 ≤ t ≤ 3`.

### **Theoretical Framework and Rules**

Geometric transformations map algebraic functions through continuous spatial scaling, reflections, and translations. A horizontal shift parameterized as ![](../assets/image40.png) translates the trajectory along the abscissa (counter-intuitively, ![](../assets/image41.png) moves right), while an external shift ![](../assets/image42.png) translates it along the ordinate.6 Scaling by a constant factor ![](../assets/image43.png) in ![](../assets/image44.png) stretches or compresses the topological wave structure both vertically and horizontally.8 Furthermore, parametric equations isolate variables into independent functions of an external parameter, usually time ![](../assets/image45.png), such as ![](../assets/image46.png) and ![](../assets/image47.png).9 The fundamental utility here is that parametric representations decouple geometric dimensions. This decoupling allows mathematical models to trace complex, self-intersecting trajectories of a particle through phase space without violating the vertical line test required for standard Cartesian functions.9
